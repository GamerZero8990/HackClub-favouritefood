Types of pizza

Pizza Napoletana Traditional wood-fired pizza oven In response to the spread of fast-food and frozen pizza, the Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana (AVPN) was founded in Naples in 1984 with the support of the Campania region. Its mission is to preserve the tradition of Neapolitan pizza. Its members, pizzerias worldwide, are authorized to label their pizzas as Verace Pizza Napoletana (authentic Neapolitan pizza). The traditional production methods and the use of the correct ingredients are regularly inspected. On 9 February 2005, Pizza Napoletana was registered as a trademark within the European Union and the permitted ingredients were defined. The production of verace Pizza Napoletana artigianale (authentic handmade Neapolitan pizza) as a specialità tradizionale garantita (STG, guaranteed traditional specialty) is defined in the Italian standard UNI 10791:98 and EU Regulation 97/2010. Since February 5, 2010, the traditional composition or traditional method of production of the product has been protected as a Traditional Speciality Guaranteed (TSG). Pizza Napoletana consists of the following basic ingredients: soft wheat flour, brewer's yeast, natural drinking water, peeled tomatoes and/or small fresh tomatoes (pomodorini), sea salt or table salt, and extra virgin olive oil. Other ingredients that may be used include garlic and oregano, fresh basil, and Mozzarella di Bufala Campana PDO or Mozzarella PTS. Baking takes place exclusively in wood-fired ovens, where a baking temperature of 485 °C, essential for preparation, is reached. The cooking time must not exceed 90 seconds. A slightly thicker crust with a "leopard" pattern is also typical of Pizza Napoletana. It is produced in two versions: Pizza Marinara, with tomatoes, garlic, olive oil and oregano Pizza Margherita, with tomatoes, mozzarella (made from buffalo or cow's milk, Fior di latte), olive oil and basil. Classic variations of Neapolitan pizza, which do not necessarily have to contain tomatoes and cheese, are: Pizza aglio e olio, with garlic, olive oil and oregano Pizza con cozze, with mussels, garlic, olive oil and parsley Pizza alle vongole, with clams, tomatoes, garlic, olive oil, parsley and oregano Margherita bianca, a Margherita without tomatoes In 2020, a dispute arose between the Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana (AVPN) and the Associazione Pizzaiuoli Napoletani (APN), the association of Neapolitan pizza makers founded in 1998, regarding the use of electric ovens. The AVPN had been the first to accept an electric oven for pizza production, the Scugnizzonapoletano, developed by the Neapolitan engineer Giuseppe Carlo Russo Krauss. They argued that insisting on wood-fired ovens was outdated, as these were not accepted in many countries, which could lead to the extinction of pizza in 50 years. Furthermore, they pointed out that UNESCO had included the "art of the 'pizzaiuolo'" on its list, not the wood-fired oven itself. The APN opposed this, emphasizing its unwillingness to sacrifice the unique tradition of the wood-fired oven to economic considerations. They also argued that the use of electric ovens jeopardized the EU designation of pizza as a Specialità tradizionale garantita (STG).

Other pizza types with regional tradition Besides Neapolitan pizza, there are other types of pizza in Italy with regional traditions. These include Romana pizza, a very thin and crispy pizza, usually baked on a baking sheet. Genovese pizza, on the other hand, is a thicker pizza, more reminiscent of the original shape of flatbread and a further development of focaccia. Pizza Cilentana originates from the Cilento region and, unlike Neapolitan pizza, is baked from a mixture of soft and durum wheat. Instead of pure tomato sauce, a soffritto of olive oil, onions, and tomatoes is used, and instead of mozzarella, goat cheese or cacioricotta is used. Regional cured meats such as noglia may also be used as toppings.

Pizza variations Four Seasons Pizza Pizza bianca Calzone Pizza fritta Pizza al taglio Chicago-style pizza Pizza types categorized by topping include, for example, Pizza capricciosa, Pizza alle quattro stagioni, and pizzas named after their ingredients: Pizza al salami (with salami), Pizza ai funghi (with mushrooms), Pizza al prosciutto (with ham), Pizza al tonno (with tuna), etc. Pizza quattro formaggi is topped with four types of cheese (for example, mozzarella, Parmesan, Gorgonzola, and Pecorino), and Pizza frutti di mare is topped with seafood. Also common in Italy is pizza bianca (white pizza), a pizza variation prepared without tomato sauce. A calzone (Italian for "trousers") is a pizza where the dough is folded over the topping before baking. The traditional filling consists of ricotta, prosciutto, mushrooms, mozzarella, Parmesan, and oregano. Originally, the calzone was not baked in an oven, but rather in a pan with lard or oil, as is common in Naples with pizza fritta. Pizza al taglio ("pizza by the slice") is widespread throughout Italy; it is baked on a rectangular tray and sold in smaller pieces. It is often offered not only in pizzerias but also in bakeries.

Kosher pizza A so-called Passover pizza is made from matzo. Matzos are soaked in a mixture of beaten eggs with milk, salt, and pepper. A boiled baking tray with high sides is greased with margarine or oil, the prepared matzos are placed in it, tomato sauce is poured over them, and they are sprinkled with any herbs and grated "yellow cheese" (gevina tzehuba, a hard or semi-hard cheese). Then it is baked in a preheated oven. Kosher pizzas are especially popular during Passover because American hard cheeses, such as cheddar, are rarely aged for six months or more, and therefore meat can be eaten immediately afterward. The date on the cheese packaging indicates how old the cheese is and helps determine whether or not to wait six hours. Meat can be eaten after consuming dairy products, with the exception of hard cheeses that have been aged for more than six months.

Pizza in East Germany Pizza was also a popular specialty in East Germany. As early as 1966, several daily newspapers published various pizza recipes for people to bake at home. Especially in East Berlin and the district capitals, there were several establishments offering pizza from the 1970s onwards. For example, an HO ice cream parlor in Berlin-Weißensee sold homemade pizza from 1975. According to them, the ingredients at the time were: unsweetened yeast dough, salami, red and green bell peppers, pickles, ketchup, various spices, and everything was topped with cheese. Freshly prepared pizza was offered at the Christmas market in East Berlin at Alexanderplatz from 1976 onwards. In September 1979, pizza production began at the Bako-Berlin combine, VEB Libelle division, offering five varieties. Initially, these were supplied to the Konsum and HO department stores in East Berlin. However, pizza dough was also purchased there by restaurants. Depending on the variety, the pizzas were topped with meat, sausage, apples, tomatoes, peppers, eggs, cheese, or nuts. A frozen pizza production plant went into operation at VEB Bako-Berlin in April 1981. Approximately 600 pizzas were baked, cooled, frozen, and then packaged in foil and cardboard boxes while still frozen every hour. From 1980 onwards, 1300 tons of pizza were produced there annually. In the early 1980s, some restaurants and pastry shops also began offering freshly prepared pizza. A pastry shop in Berlin-Weißensee was known for selling a round pizza. This creation consisted of a spicy tomato sauce and was topped with cervelat sausage, onions, various spices, tomatoes, and cheese. The Vierlanden restaurant in Berlin-Marzahn, which operated its own pizzeria, was also well-known. In 1987, the Margarethe anniversary pizza was launched to celebrate the 750th anniversary of Berlin. From 1988, the VEB Libelle factory in Berlin-Biesdorf produced frozen pizzas in ten different varieties for retailers. In East German restaurants, a unique pizza variation was also created. The dish was called Krusta, baked on a baking sheet, and cut into rectangular pieces about the size of a hand. It was developed by a collective of young chefs and presented to a wide audience at the "Masters of Tomorrow" trade fair in Leipzig in November 1976. However, Krusta was not intended to replace pizza itself or even its name. The crust dough was darker because it contained rye flour in addition to wheat flour. Toppings included products that were readily available year-round in East Germany: salami, bacon, beer ham, eggs, minced meat, chicken, fish, lecsó (a vegetable stew), and sauerkraut, but also anchovies and Cuban pineapple. The Krusta was served in a specially established fast-food restaurant called Krusta-Stube. From 1986 onwards, there was a fast-food chain called Pizza-Büfett.

frozen pizza Pre-baked and frozen pizza is among the best-selling convenience foods. Developed in the United States in the 1960s, it spread to Europe via Italy. In 1966, the Italian ice cream manufacturer Motta presented a "mini pizza" along with a corresponding baking oven at German trade fairs. The production of frozen pizza differs in sequence from traditional preparation. The die-cut dough rounds are first spread with tomato sauce and partially baked, then topped with the remaining ingredients after cooling, and finally flash-frozen. The dough may contain modified starch and additional leavening agents such as sodium bicarbonate, in addition to wheat flour and yeast, which allows baking without prior slow thawing. Both during partial baking and final baking in a household oven, the temperatures are far lower than those of a pizza oven. Frozen pizza was first produced in large quantities in 1968 by the bakery manufacturer Romano Freddi from Mantua. He developed the fundamentals for shaping the dough, topping, and pre-baking for large-scale industrial production. Besides the Italian company Esselunga, he also supplied Dr. Oetker, the company that was the first to introduce frozen pizza to the German market. Also in 1968, the first manufacturers in Switzerland followed, and in 1970, the first in Germany, including Wagner, which is now one of the leading frozen pizza manufacturers in Europe with a market share of over 30% in Germany and 26% in Europe. Wagner also launched the first frozen "stone-baked pizza" in 1976. The largest manufacturer in Europe is Freiberger Lebensmittel GmbH, which emerged from a pizza mail-order bakery in 1976. Standard frozen pizza in foil Sales of frozen pizza in Germany increased rapidly: 2,800 tons were produced in 1973, and 277,000 tons in 2020. According to Dr. Oetker, their best-selling varieties in 2017 were salami, Speciale (salami, ham, mushrooms), tuna, Hawaii (ham, pineapple), and Margherita. In addition to pre-topped pizzas, there are also products whose dough is not topped or pre-baked, but rather cooked in the consumer's oven. Tuna pizzas, for example, generally do not use true tuna, but rather the non-endangered skipjack tuna (Katsuwonus pelamis), which does not belong to the genus Thunnus. The managing director of the German Frozen Food Institute, Sabine Eichner, classifies frozen pizza as a "lifestyle product." From January to June 2021, frozen pizzas generated sales of €1.3 billion. Of this, more than €1 billion was generated by branded products. As another convenience food option besides frozen pizza, retailers offer pizza kits in the refrigerated section. These usually contain the dough already rolled out and on baking paper, which can then be topped with the included tomato sauce and separately purchased toppings.
